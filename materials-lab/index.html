Colour from Spanish earth

Our first cultural research project. Painter Katya is building a collection of natural pigments made only from Spanish earth — minerals, soils and plants — and testing how they behave on paper and on clay.

The project

What we're researching and why

Before paint came in tubes, colour came from the ground. Ochres, umbers, green and blue minerals, roots, leaves and flowers were gathered, ground and washed by hand. The painter's knowledge was inseparable from the land.

In the Pigment Lab, Katya sets out to gather as complete a collection of natural pigments as she can, using only what Spanish earth gives — minerals and plants. Starting with a course in traditional pigment extraction, she then collects material across the Valencian region, Granada and Andalusia, and Galicia.

The lab works for both paper and ceramics. Pigments that don't survive the kiln still find a use on paper. The aim is the widest possible palette, and a way to pass this knowledge on, so other artists can make their own colours instead of buying synthetic or laboratory ones.

Research question
"Which pigments can be made from the minerals, earths and plants of Spain without synthetic additives — and how do they behave on paper and in ceramics?"
1

What the land holds

Which minerals, earths and plants in each region can give colour.

2

On paper

How each pigment works on paper, including how well it holds up to light.

3

In ceramics

Which minerals work on clay through firing, beyond the familiar iron oxide.

What Season 1 produces

Nobody knows in advance how many pigments the land will give. Finding that out is the research. So the result isn't a number, it's:

  • — A map of sources, including the places and materials that didn't give a pigment
  • — A pigment library, with brushstrokes and test pieces on paper and on clay
  • — A written-down method, so others can repeat it
  • — Unexpected textures and "defects", which count as results too
  • — Two public events: a working lab day in May 2027 and a final exhibition in November 2027
Methodology

The process, step by step

Every material goes through the same steps, so results can be compared and shared. The base method comes from Katya's November 2026 course at the King's Foundation School of Traditional Arts.

  1. 1

    Source

    Collect the mineral, earth or plant and record where it came from.

  2. 2

    Extract

    Grind and wash minerals; extract colour from plants.

  3. 3

    Refine

    Settle out and dry the finest pigment.

  4. 4

    Paper

    Make paint, test brushstrokes and light-fastness on paper.

  5. 5

    Clay

    Paint test pieces and fire them; record how the colour changes.

  6. 6

    Record

    Add the result to the map and library — including what didn't work.

Safety and care. Wet grinding is used throughout to minimise dust. Toxic or suspect minerals are excluded from the palette. All painted ceramic pieces produced in the lab are decorative only and not suitable for food use.

November 2026 kickoff course

Season 1 begins

Pigment Lab is Saged Club's first cultural research project, and it starts this month. Painter Katya is spending the next year collecting colour from Spanish earth — minerals, soils and plants — and learning how each one behaves on paper and on clay.

It starts with learning. In November Katya studies pigment extraction at the King's Foundation School of Traditional Arts, working with both minerals and plants. That course gives the base method she'll use for every material after it.

Then the collecting begins: the Valencian region this winter, Granada and Andalusia in spring, Galicia in summer. Everything found, and everything that doesn't work, goes onto the map.
